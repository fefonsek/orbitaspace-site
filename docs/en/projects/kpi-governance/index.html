One number, three answers: KPI governance at Vallora Logística

KPI governance
KPIs
Management
R
Three departments, three absenteeism rates for the same month. How 40 KPIs became 14, each with a definition sheet, an owner and a change rule.

Fictional case. Vallora Logística S.A., its departments, people and KPIs do not exist. The three absenteeism rates were calculated from the fictional dataset of the Absenteeism Radar; the inventory, the definition sheets and the rules were written for this case. The method is real.

Download the three monthly rates (CSV) · Download the KPI inventory (CSV)

The CSV files and the comments in the R code are in Portuguese, the case’s original language.

The story

At the July 2025 results meeting, Vallora heard three absenteeism rates for June. HR showed 4.38%, Operations 3.54% and Finance 2.15%, and the meeting spent half an hour arguing about which one was right.

None of them was wrong. Each department calculated what it needed for its own work and gave the result the same name. The problem was not arithmetic, it was governance: nobody had written down what “absenteeism rate” meant at the company, or who got to decide.

The scene repeated itself over the following months, and in December leadership asked for an inventory of every KPI that circulated in the monthly presentations. Forty turned up, from six departments, and only 13 were well defined. The work took five weeks, in four stages:

  1. Interviews with the people who produce the numbers and the people who use them, in all six departments.
  2. Inventory and diagnosis of each KPI.
  3. Definition sheets for the KPIs that go to leadership.
  4. Governance rules: who proposes, who approves, how a change is recorded.

The three rates come from the same data and answer different questions. The decision was to keep all three, each with its own name, and reserve “Absenteeism rate” for HR’s definition, which is the same as the Radar’s.

Diagnosis: from 40 to 14

Of the 40 KPIs, 14 go to the leadership meeting, 14 stay with the departments and 12 leave the presentations, merged or discontinued. Each KPI was given a status and a destination, using the criteria below.

Status found Criterion Count
Well defined Written formula, single source, an owner, and used in some decision 13
Inconsistent Same name, different calculations across departments or sites 7
Duplicate Different names for the same measure, or one is a slice of the other 7
Poorly designed Volume with no reference point, a slice treated as a KPI, or the wrong incentive 6
Unused Not part of any decision recorded in the minutes in the last six months 4
No owner Nobody answers for the result or the calculation 3
Destination What it means Count
Leadership Goes to the monthly meeting, with a full definition sheet, a target and an owner 14
Department level The department tracks it with a short definition sheet; it goes to leadership only by exception 14
Merged Becomes part of another KPI or a breakdown of it 6
Discontinued Leaves the presentations; the data remain available 6

To go to leadership, a KPI has to pass three questions: it is tied to a company objective, someone in leadership makes a decision with it, and it moves enough from one month to the next to deserve the meeting.

The most instructive case is “Days without accidents”. It looks positive, but it rewards not reporting accidents; the Accident frequency rate measures the same thing without that incentive.

Two details that show the method: Warehouse occupancy has no “higher is better”, because a warehouse that is too full jams the operation; and 90-day turnover only closes three months after hiring, so the sheet says so, and nobody asks for the number before it exists.

Governance: who decides what each number means

No definition changes without a request, a test and an approval on record. The decision belongs to a KPI committee: the financial controller chairs it, the owners of the 14 KPIs take part, and the BI analyst keeps the minutes. It meets once a month, for 30 minutes, only when there are requests in the queue.

How a definition changes

  1. The owner opens a request: what changes, why, and from when.
  2. BI recalculates the last 12 months under the new rule and shows the difference.
  3. The committee approves, rejects or asks for changes at its next meeting.
  4. Once approved, BI updates the definition sheet, bumps the version and logs the change.
  5. The history is recalculated; when that is not possible, the break is marked on the chart.

Results

The monthly meeting traded six presentations and 40 numbers for a single page with 14 KPIs, each with its owner in the room to explain it. When Operations talks about its rate, it now goes by another name, and the July argument does not happen again. The Absenteeism Radar now uses the official PES-01 definition.

What this case demonstrates

  • Separating the math from the governance. A divergence is rarely a calculation error; it is almost always the lack of a written definition and of someone who decides.
  • Cutting without deleting. Twenty-six KPIs left the leadership meeting, and no department lost information it needs.
  • Definition sheets that answer the questions that start arguments: what goes in, what stays out, whether higher is better, when the number closes.
