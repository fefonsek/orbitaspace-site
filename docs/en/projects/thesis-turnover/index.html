Fictional consulting case for an undergraduate thesis: from the survey to the thesis tables, with the whole analysis in R.

Fictional data. The student, the supermarkets and the 230 responses do not exist. The data were simulated in R with programmed patterns, for demonstration purposes, and were deliberately given problems that are common in online surveys. The results show the working method; they are not a finding about real supermarkets.

The request

A Business Administration student was finishing an undergraduate thesis titled Leadership, recognition and turnover intention among supermarket employees in central-western Minas Gerais. He ran a Google Forms survey in stores across the region: profile questions plus 20 statements rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), in five blocks.

Block Items Sample statement
Leadership 5 “My supervisor treats me with respect.”
Recognition 4 “I get praise for my work.”
Perceived pay 4 “My pay is fair for the work I do.”
Work-life balance 4 “I can plan my days off in advance.”
Turnover intention 3 “I plan to look for another job in the next 12 months.”

The project had four hypotheses:

  • H1. Better-rated leadership reduces the intention to leave.
  • H2. More recognition reduces the intention to leave.
  • H3. Better-rated pay reduces the intention to leave, with a smaller effect than H1 and H2.
  • H4. Better work-life balance reduces the intention to leave.

He had the spreadsheet exported from Forms and his advisor’s instruction to “do the statistics part.” He needed to know what to run, in what order, how to check whether he could trust the results and how to report everything in the thesis.

Of the 230 responses received, 212 went on to the analysis. Each exclusion has a reason that can be written into the thesis methods section:

Someone who gives the same answer to everything, including the reversed leadership item (“My supervisor rarely gives me feedback”), is saying at the same time that the supervisor does and does not give feedback: a sign they did not read the statements.

The valid responses still had 87 scattered blank items (2.1% of cells). The rule was simple and is written into the report: each block’s score is the mean of the items answered, as long as no more than 1 item in the block is blank. Two people left 2 Work-life balance items blank and have no score for that block.

3. Scale reliability

Before adding statements up into a score, you have to show that they measure the same thing. I used two indicators: Cronbach’s alpha, which the advisor and the thesis committee expect to see, and McDonald’s omega, which is better suited when the statements do not all carry the same weight in the construct. Above .70 is the usual threshold.

Show the R code 
confiab <- function(cols) {
  x <- select(d, all_of(cols))
  c(alfa = suppressWarnings(psych::alpha(x)$total$raw_alpha),
    omega = suppressMessages(suppressWarnings(psych::omega(x, nfactors = 1, plot = FALSE)$omega.tot)))
}
rem_sem4 <- c("REM1", "REM2", "REM3")
conf <- bind_rows(
  lapply(names(blocos), function(b) c(bloco = b, itens = length(blocos[[b]]), confiab(blocos[[b]]))),
  c(bloco = "REM_sem4", itens = 3, confiab(rem_sem4))
) |>
  mutate(across(c(itens, alfa, omega), as.numeric)) |>
  arrange(match(bloco, c("LID", "REC", "REM", "REM_sem4", "EQU", "INT")))

# Correlação de cada item de Remuneração com o total dos outros três.
r_rem <- psych::alpha(select(d, all_of(blocos$REM)))$item.stats$r.drop
names(r_rem) <- blocos$REM

Four blocks passed comfortably. Perceived pay came in at .68 because of one item: “I receive my transportation allowance on time” (REM4) barely moves with the other three (a correlation of .09 with their total, against .58 or more for the rest). That makes sense: it measures whether a benefit arrives on time, not whether the pay feels fair. Without it, alpha rises to .82. REM4 was dropped from the Perceived pay score, and the thesis records that decision and the reason for it.

4. Exploratory factor analysis

Factor analysis checks whether the 20 statements group into the five blocks the questionnaire intended. Because the answers are ordinal (1 to 5), I used polychoric correlation, which treats each answer as a band on an underlying continuous scale and does not assume that the distance between “1” and “2” equals the distance between “4” and “5”.

Show the R code 
x_itens <- select(d, all_of(cod))
pc <- suppressWarnings(polychoric(x_itens)$rho)
kmo <- KMO(pc)$MSA
bartlett <- cortest.bartlett(pc, n = nrow(d))
fe <- suppressWarnings(fa(x_itens, nfactors = 5, cor = "poly", rotate = "oblimin", fm = "minres"))

cargas <- unclass(fe$loadings)
# Dá a cada fator o código do bloco cujos itens carregam mais nele.
dominante <- sapply(seq_len(ncol(cargas)), function(f) {
  names(which.max(tapply(abs(cargas[, f]), substr(rownames(cargas), 1, 3), mean)))
})
colnames(cargas) <- dominante
cargas <- cargas[, c("LID", "REC", "REM", "EQU", "INT")]
carga_rem4 <- max(abs(cargas["REM4", ]))

The KMO was .84 (above .80 is considered very good) and Bartlett’s test gave χ²(190) = 2,198.0, p < .001: the correlations between the statements are strong enough for factor analysis. With five factors and an oblique rotation (the constructs are allowed to correlate), each statement loaded on its own block, with one exception we already knew about: REM4 did not load on any factor (highest loading 0.14), which confirms the reliability decision.

5. Scores and group comparisons

escore <- function(cols) {

d <- d |>

Likert scale scores tend to be skewed and full of ties, so I compared the intention to leave across groups with non-parametric tests, always reporting the effect size, which tells you whether a difference is large or merely statistically detectable.

  • Shift. Night-shift workers have a higher intention to leave (Mann-Whitney, p < .001, r = .25, a small effect).
  • Tenure. The groups differ (Kruskal-Wallis, H(3) = 10.1, p = .018, ε² = .034, a small effect). In the pairwise comparisons (Dunn, with Holm correction), the difference that holds up is between “up to 1 year” and “1 to 3 years”; the other comparisons were not significant.

6. Correlations

Show the R code 
ct <- corr.test(select(d, LID, REC, REM, EQU, INT), method = "spearman", adjust = "none")
rho <- ct$r

Spearman correlation (appropriate for ordinal scores) shows all four blocks associated with the intention to leave, all in the expected direction (p = .002 or lower). The blocks are also correlated with one another, which matters for the next step.

7. Multiple regression

Correlation looks at one factor at a time. To test the hypotheses, the question is different: how much does each factor matter when the others are in the same model? I used multiple linear regression with the four scores standardized (so the effects are comparable) and three controls: night shift, up to 1 year of tenure and sex.

Show the R code 
dm <- d |>
  transmute(INT = INT, LID = LID, REC = REC, REM = REM, EQU = EQU,
            noturno = as.numeric(turno == "Noite"),
            novato = as.numeric(tempo_casa == "Até 1 ano"),
            feminino = as.numeric(sexo == "Feminino")) |>
  drop_na() |>
  mutate(across(c(INT, LID, REC, REM, EQU), \(v) as.numeric(scale(v))))
modelo <- lm(INT ~ LID + REC + REM + EQU + noturno + novato + feminino, data = dm)

# Pressupostos
vifs <- vif(modelo)
bp <- bptest(modelo)
sw <- shapiro.test(residuals(modelo))
cook_max <- max(cooks.distance(modelo))

# Erros-padrão robustos (HC3): protegem os testes se a variância dos resíduos não for constante.
v_hc3 <- vcovHC(modelo, type = "HC3")
rob <- coeftest(modelo, vcov. = v_hc3)
ic <- coefci(modelo, vcov. = v_hc3)
resultado <- tibble(termo = rownames(rob), beta = rob[, 1], ep = rob[, 2], p = rob[, 4],
                    li = ic[, 1], ls = ic[, 2]) |>
  filter(termo != "(Intercept)")
ajuste <- summary(modelo)
# H3 diz que o efeito da remuneração é menor: testa se os coeficientes são iguais.
igual_lid <- linearHypothesis(modelo, "REM = LID", vcov. = v_hc3)
igual_rec <- linearHypothesis(modelo, "REM = REC", vcov. = v_hc3)
teste <- function(h) c(F = h$F[2], gl1 = h$Df[2], gl2 = h$Res.Df[2], p = h$`Pr(>F)`[2])
menor_confirmado <- igual_lid$`Pr(>F)`[2] < 0.05 && igual_rec$`Pr(>F)`[2] < 0.05
# O texto das páginas foi escrito para o caso em que a diferença não se confirma.
stopifnot(!menor_confirmado)

# H3 tem duas partes: reduz (testada pelo β) e com efeito menor (testada acima).
hipoteses <- tibble(termo = c("LID", "REC", "REM", "EQU")) |>
  left_join(resultado, by = "termo") |>
  mutate(decisao = case_when(!(beta < 0 & p < 0.05) ~ "nao",
                             termo == "REM" & !menor_confirmado ~ "parcial",
                             TRUE ~ "sim"))

Before reading the coefficients, I checked the assumptions:

  • Multicollinearity: highest VIF of 1.47, well below the usual limit of 5. The blocks are correlated, but not to the point of being confounded.
  • Residual variance: Breusch-Pagan test, p = .713. No strong sign of non-constant variance; even so, the tests use robust standard errors (HC3), which cost little and protect the result.
  • Normality of residuals: Shapiro-Wilk, p = .049. With more than 200 cases, a slight departure does not compromise the coefficient tests.
  • Influential cases: highest Cook’s distance of 0.04, far below 1. No single response changes the result.

The model explains 43% of the variation in the intention to leave (adjusted R² = .41, N = 210).

H3 says the effect of pay is smaller than those of leadership and recognition. Looking at the βs is not enough: you have to test whether the difference between them is real. The test of equality of coefficients (with the same HC3 correction) did not confirm the difference: F(1, 202) = 3.02, p = .084 against leadership and F(1, 202) = 1.92, p = .167 against recognition.

grafico_likert <- function(res, tx) {
  enunciados <- if (is.null(tx$enunciados)) setNames(res$itens$enunciado, res$itens$codigo) else tx$enunciados

  inv <- if_else(res$itens$invertido == "sim", paste0(" (", tx$invertido, ")"), "")

  • Work-life balance is the most instructive result. In the simple correlation it appears associated with the intention to leave (ρ = −.22), but in the model the effect drops to zero (β = 0.04, p = .468). Work-life balance goes hand in hand with leadership and recognition: where the supervisor is good, days off tend to be planned. On its own, it “borrows” the effect of the others.

To test the hypotheses, a multiple linear regression with robust standard errors (HC3) was estimated, with turnover intention as the dependent variable. The model explained 43% of the variance (adjusted R² = .41). Perceived leadership (β = −0.31, p < .001) and recognition (β = −0.30, p < .001) were negatively associated with turnover intention, supporting H1 and H2. Perceived pay was also negatively associated with it (β = −0.16, p = .004). Although its estimate was smaller than those for leadership and recognition, tests of equality of coefficients did not indicate a significant difference (F(1, 202) = 3.02, p = .084, and F(1, 202) = 1.92, p = .167, respectively); H3 was therefore partially supported. Work-life balance was not associated with turnover intention after controlling for the other variables (β = 0.04, p = .468); H4 was not supported.
